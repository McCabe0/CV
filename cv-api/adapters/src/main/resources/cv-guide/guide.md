# What makes a strong tech CV

Distilled, in our own words, from *The Tech Resume Inside Out* by Gergely Orosz (2020). No text of the
book is copied here; read it for the full advice. The writer drafts to this guide, and the critic scores
against it. The rule above every other: a CV says only what the candidate's profile and answers say.

## Who reads it

- A recruiter skims it first, for seconds: role titles, companies, dates, the main technologies. Make
  these easy to find at a glance.
- A hiring manager reads it properly, later: what the candidate did, how well, and what came of it.
- Screening software may filter it by keywords from the job before any person sees it.

## Ground rules

- Simple and consistent: the same format for every role, every date, every bullet.
- Plain words. No internal project code names or company jargon: say what the thing was.
- No personal details beyond how to reach the candidate: no photo, age, date of birth, gender, marital
  status or nationality. They invite bias and add nothing.
- Perfect spelling and grammar.

## Order of the sections

- With work experience: a short summary, then skills, then experience, then projects, education and
  certifications.
- New graduates, bootcamp graduates and career changers with little relevant work: skills, then
  education and projects, then experience. Lead with what is strongest.
- Senior people: experience first and longest, showing scope (team size, systems owned, people led).

## Summary

- Two or three sentences, about this person and the target role. Name the years of experience, the
  focus, and the strongest piece of evidence from the profile.
- Specific, never generic. Avoid "passionate", "hard-working", "team player", "results-driven",
  "self-starter" and the like: they could describe anyone.
- No ambitions the role may not offer, eg wanting to lead when applying as an individual contributor.

## Skills

- The languages and technologies the candidate can work with that matter for the target role,
  strongest first. Order carries the emphasis.
- No self-ratings: no stars, percentages or "expert" labels.
- Not every technology ever touched. A long list of everything hides what matters and reads as
  keyword stuffing.

## Experience

- Most recent first. Recent roles get the most detail (three to six bullets); older ones fewer; roles
  from 10 to 15 years ago one line, or none.
- Show progression: a promotion inside one company is two titles with their dates. Explain an unusual
  title in plain words.
- Dates easy to scan, in one format. Months for recent roles; years alone are fine for old ones.

## Bullets

- What was achieved, not what the job was. "Responsible for the payments service" says nothing;
  "Cut failed payments by 30% by adding retries with backoff" says what came of the work.
- A useful shape: the result, how it was measured, and what the candidate did to get it. Not every
  bullet needs all three, but most should have a result.
- Numbers make a bullet stand out: users, requests, latency, revenue, time saved, team size, tickets,
  coverage. Use a number only where the profile or the answers give it. Never invent or estimate one.
- Active verbs in the past tense: led, built, cut, moved, shipped, replaced, mentored.
- The candidate's own contribution. Not "we" or "the team": say what they did within the team.
- Name the main technology near the end of the bullet where it helps, matching the skills section.
- One idea a bullet, rarely more than two lines. No bullets under bullets.

## Tailoring to the role

- Use the words the target role uses for the same things, where the profile supports them, eg "backend"
  and "distributed systems" for someone who built services and message queues.
- Put the role's key technologies, those the candidate has, in the skills section and in the bullets
  where they were used. This is what screening software looks for.
- Do not stuff in keywords the candidate's experience does not back.

## Projects

- Side projects, open source or study projects that are relevant, complex or show impact: what it is,
  what it does, and what came of it (users, stars, a result).

## Leave out

- A spoken languages section, unless the role or the market needs a language other than the CV's own.
- References, or "references on request".
- Hobbies, unless they bear on the role.
- More than four ways to contact or links: email, phone, LinkedIn, and one portfolio or code link.

## Experience levels and paths

- New graduates: education, coursework and projects carry the CV; include internships and hackathons.
- Bootcamp graduates and career changers: show the projects built, and the results from the earlier
  career that transfer, eg leading people, working with customers, measurable outcomes.
- A career break: one honest line, if the profile gives it.
- Senior and lead roles: scope and influence, eg systems owned, people mentored or managed, decisions
  that changed how a team worked.

## What the critic checks

- Every employer, title, date, number, skill and qualification is in the profile or the answers.
- Bullets show results, with numbers where the profile has them.
- The target role's keywords appear where the profile supports them.
- The summary is specific to this person and this role.
- The ground rules above: no personal details, no jargon, no self-ratings, nothing from "Leave out".
